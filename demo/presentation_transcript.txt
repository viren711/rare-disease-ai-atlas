RARE DISEASE AI ATLAS: PRESENTATION TRANSCRIPT (10 slides)
==========================================================
Slides: demo/slides.pdf (or open demo/slides/slides.html in a browser).
Length: about 6 minutes at a calm pace (about 900 words). Speakers rotate: Viren (1-3), Ninad (4-6), Mayukh (7-9), Viren (10).


SLIDE 1: Title (Viren, ~25 s)
-----------------------------
Good morning. We are Team Arjun from IIT Bombay: I'm Viren, with Ninad and Mayukh. Our project for Challenge 05 is the
Rare Disease AI Atlas. In one line: it finds the dots nobody connected. It takes a patient group from a single diagnosis to
related diseases, the research that already exists, the people working on it, and a next step, with the evidence behind
every link.


SLIDE 2: The problem (Viren, ~40 s)
-----------------------------------
There are about ten thousand rare diseases. Around eighty percent are genetic, more than three hundred million people live
with one, and fewer than five percent have an approved treatment. Three things make progress slow. The knowledge is
scattered across databases, papers, trial registries, grants and patient groups. Diseases are filed by name, so two
diseases that share a pathway look unrelated. And communities rebuild registries and studies that another group already
has. As Maria, the patient leader in the brief, puts it: her family may be alone with this diagnosis, but a pathway could
connect them to another community.


SLIDE 3: The solution (Viren, ~40 s)
------------------------------------
Our answer is one knowledge graph, organised by mechanism and symptoms instead of names, that always ends in an action.
It answers the brief's three questions: who shares our disease characteristics, what useful work already exists, and what
we should do together next. Every link carries its evidence: its source, date, confidence and a stance, from supported to
unsupported. Database records, findings from papers and links the atlas computed are drawn differently, so a hypothesis is
never mistaken for a fact. And when nothing is supported, the atlas says so and tells you what to ask next.


SLIDE 4: Dataset (Ninad, ~35 s)
-------------------------------
The graph is built from ten public sources. MONDO gives stable disease identifiers. The Human Phenotype Ontology gives
symptoms, and across almost thirteen thousand diseases it tells us how rare each symptom is. Orphanet adds genes and
prevalence, Reactome adds biological pathways, and ClinVar adds variants. For research we use twenty-six thousand PubMed
abstracts with PubTator annotations, thirteen hundred studies from ClinicalTrials.gov and about eighteen hundred NIH grants.
Our demo slice is lysosomal and peroxisomal diseases, two families that leukodystrophy bridges.


SLIDE 5: Preprocessing (Ninad, ~45 s)
-------------------------------------
The pipeline has six steps. We download everything in parallel, with resumable, rate-limited scripts. We resolve names, so
OMIM, Orphanet and MeSH identifiers and every synonym map to one node. We connect them into typed edges, and each edge
stores its source, date, confidence and evidence type. We score disease similarity: rare shared symptoms count for half,
shared pathways for thirty percent and co-mentioning papers for twenty, and Louvain clustering groups the diseases. We
attach a confidence score and flag possible counter-evidence. Finally we index every abstract for search. The result is
about sixty-one thousand nodes and two hundred and seven thousand edges, rebuilt in thirty-five seconds, with no language
model needed to build it.


SLIDE 6: Architecture (Ninad, ~30 s)
------------------------------------
The system has four layers. The data layer is plain Parquet tables and a search index. The atlas engine loads the graph into
memory with NetworkX and handles neighbours, evidence, synonyms, action plans and plain-language explanations. The
literature layer retrieves, reranks, checks grounding and verifies citations. Everything meets at one contract, api.py,
which is the only thing the interface talks to. That separation lets us test each layer on its own.


SLIDE 7: Tech stack (Mayukh, ~35 s)
-----------------------------------
The whole stack is open source and runs on a CPU with no API keys. Data and graph work use Python, pandas, Parquet,
NetworkX and scikit-learn. Search uses bge-small embeddings with BM25, FAISS and a cross-encoder reranker. The language
model is Qwen 2.5 three-billion, four-bit, on llama.cpp. The interface is Streamlit. Running locally keeps it private and free,
and because the model client uses the OpenAI-compatible API, a hosted model can be plugged in with one setting. We did not
train or fine-tune any model.


SLIDE 8: User interface (Mayukh, ~35 s)
---------------------------------------
The interface follows one rule: summary first, evidence on click. Home has one search box, three entry points for different
users, and a guided demo. Explore shows the disease card, similar diseases with the reason for each, an interactive map and
an evidence panel beside every link. The action plan gives readiness, caveats, shared studies, people to contact and a
proposal to send. Ask the literature answers in plain language with citations, or refuses clearly. There are light and dark
themes, and node types differ by shape as well as colour.


SLIDE 9: Deployment and tools (Mayukh, ~35 s)
---------------------------------------------
It runs on one laptop with six cores and no GPU. A single start command launches the model server and the app. It stays up
inside a tmux session with automatic restarts and a health watchdog, and a Cloudflare tunnel makes it reachable over HTTPS
without opening any ports. One script rebuilds the data, graph and index from public sources. For testing we used Streamlit's
app tests, stubbed-model smoke tests and Playwright in a real browser, plus ffmpeg for the demo video, Git, and Claude Code
as an AI coding assistant.


SLIDE 10: Results and next steps (Viren, ~40 s)
-----------------------------------------------
We measured it. The search found the right abstract in the top five for every precise question, twenty-eight of thirty-two
end-to-end answers were correct, every off-topic or unanswerable question was refused, and nine of ten plain-language
rewrites passed our fact checks. We are open about the limits: researchers without an ORCID are matched by name,
counter-evidence flags are heuristics, and funding coverage is NIH only. Next, we want more disease groups, evidence
contributed by patient groups, and a shared natural history study as our first ten-times milestone. From an isolated
diagnosis to a shared path toward treatment. Thank you.
